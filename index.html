01Abstract

We studied which visible content pages under-capture clicks relative to their search position — CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring, one of four predefined lanes in the FlyRank ML Internship Program. The method: score each page's gap between its observed click-through rate and a position-tier-adjusted expectation, computed only from volume-gated (500+ monthly impressions) pages to avoid noise from low-traffic outliers. We built a transparent rule-based baseline, then trained decision-tree and random-forest models on five non-label-derived features, validated with a client-grouped split and a genuine forward test against the following month's real outcome. The rule outperformed both models by a wide margin (precision@30 of 0.433 versus 0.100 and 0.000, against a 0.002 base rate) — because the rule is allowed to measure March's real CTR directly, and the models are deliberately not. The result is a ranked, human-reviewed action queue with named limitations, not a production system.

02Introduction

The decision this work supports: which pages should a content reviewer, with limited weekly capacity, check first?

A flat rule like "low CTR is bad" doesn't survive contact with real search data — a 1% click-through rate is excellent at position 15 and poor at position 2. The useful question isn't a page's raw CTR, but whether it's converting worse than other pages that share its position and visibility level. That reframing — from "predict CTR" to "rank pages by how far they fall short of a fair comparison group" — is what turns this into a ranking problem rather than a classification problem, and it's the frame this entire study is built on.

A plain per-position lookup already answers part of this. The open question this study actually tests is narrower and more honest: does a learned model, using only structural signals a reviewer could check before waiting for a full month of click data, rank pages better than the plain rule does? The answer, tested against a real forward outcome rather than a same-period fit, is no — and the reason why is a finding in its own right.

03Data

All analysis in this study uses the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release, hosted on Hugging Face (gated, instant approval). The primary table is fact_content_daily_performance, partitioned by month; this study's main window is March 2026 (9,841,378 page-days, filtered to gsc_data_available IS TRUE), aggregated to one row per page for the month — 175,304 pages after requiring at least one valid daily position reading.

A second, independent pull of April 2026 served as the forward-validation window in the Results section below — never used in training, only to check what actually happened next.

What was deliberately excluded, and why

  • Client and content identity — everything here runs on the dataset's own pseudonymized client_hash_id / content_hash_id pair. No client name, domain, URL, or search query appears anywhere in this study or its underlying notebooks.
  • gsc_clicks and the resulting ctr_pct as model features — these define the target itself; including them collapsed a model's test fit from an honest 0.622 to a hollow 0.997 (see Methodology).
  • gsc_sum_position — a raw, unnormalized sum, not comparable across pages with different impression counts.
  • The fact_content_daily_performance_sample table for anything except query-mechanics testing — it turned out to be entirely June 2026 data, not a random cross-section, so using it to develop March logic would have been circular.
  • Any FlyRank product flag (health score, priority flags) — not present in this release by design, and would encode an existing decision rather than an independent signal if it were.

04Methodology

Target definition

For each page-month, ctr_gap = the position tier's expected CTR (the median CTR among visible pages — 500+ monthly impressions — in that tier) minus the page's own observed CTR. A positive gap means the page is underperforming its tier. Restricting the expected-CTR calculation to visible pages mattered: computed on the full, unfiltered page set, median CTR came back as exactly 0.0 in every position tier — a zero-inflation artifact from low-volume pages with no real clicks, not evidence that position and CTR are unrelated. Features

Five, deliberately capped: avg_position (impression-weighted; days with no position data excluded rather than treated as position zero), total_impressions, spike_day_share (share of the month's impressions concentrated in anomalous traffic-spike days — introduced after it surfaced as a real weakness in the rule's own top picks, see Results), ga4_engaged_sessions, and sessions_organic. None are derived from gsc_clicks or ctr_pct.

Baseline rule

One transparent, hand-readable score: visible × total_impressions × max(ctr_gap, 0) — a page must clear the volume gate, and only a real (positive) gap counts, weighted by how much traffic is riding on it. One reason code (ctr_below_position_expectation), one action label (review_title_meta).

Validation design

Two honesty checks, run directly against each other:

  • Split: grouped by client (80/20), not a random row split. A random split showed 44 overlapping clients between train and test and a test R² of 0.622; the grouped split, with zero overlap, gave 0.596 — a real if modest 0.026 gap attributable to partial client memorization.
  • Leakage: deliberately adding total_clicks back into the feature set collapsed the honest 0.622 R² up to 0.997 — the textbook signature of a label-derived feature. Removed, the score returns to 0.622.
  • Forward test: the real evaluation metric is precision@30, scored not against March's own gap but against April's independently-computed outcome (April's own position-tier baseline, its own gap, its own impact) — a genuine test of whether March's ranking predicts something that hadn't happened yet.

Why the rule wins, honestly: the rule directly measures March's actual CTR to compute its gap. The models were deliberately never shown ctr_pct or gsc_clicks — the same leakage rule that protects the study elsewhere also removes the one signal that would let a model match the rule's information advantage. The models are genuinely forecasting from structure alone; losing to direct measurement is expected, not a defect in the models.

The gap between the two models is itself worth reading: the depth-limited decision tree scored below the random base rate, suggesting it split on too little signal to rank meaningfully at that depth. The random forest's ensemble averaging recovered real lift — fifty times the base rate — but still far short of direct measurement.

A weakness the rule's own top picks revealed

Manually reviewing the rule's highest-ranked pages surfaced a real failure mode: several of the top-scoring pages owed their "premium position" almost entirely to a handful of anomalous traffic-spike days, not a representative month. In the most extreme case, 99.9% of one page's monthly impressions arrived in a 4-day cluster with a near-zero recorded position, while the other 27 days of the month showed an ordinary position in the 5–18 range. That finding became spike_day_share, a feature in every subsequent model, and a required human-review check in the final playbook — a rule this simple has to be watched, not trusted blindly.

Auditing the source research paper the same way

Applying the same scrutiny to FlyRank's own portfolio-level research paper (March 2026) surfaced two genuine methodology questions, asked constructively:

  • Its Random Forest feature-importance chart for Health Score ranks Average Position (43%) and Impressions (32%) highest — but Health Score is literally defined as a weighted sum that includes both. The paper discloses this ("high importance is therefore expected"), which is the right instinct; the sharper follow-up is what the ranking looks like restricted to features that aren't components of the label.
  • Its growth classifier reports 71% holdout accuracy without stating the base rate. The paper's own counts (74.8K growing vs. 45.6K declining pages) put the majority-class base rate at 62.1% — an 8.9-point real lift, not 71 points of skill.

06Limitations & honest framing

Everything in this study is observed, associated, or decision-support — never causal. This is cross-sectional, observational data from one lane, one primary month (March 2026), forward-checked against one subsequent month (April 2026). Specific, named limits:

  • A page ranking high on this queue is associated with a gap versus its position-tier peers. Fixing it is not guaranteed, by this data, to close that gap — no experiment was run.
  • spike_day_share flags roughly 3.8% of all actionable rows as needing a manual raw-data check before the position figure is trusted.
  • A clicks × CPC captured-value proxy (the source paper's own defensible formula) was tested against a real cpc field, but 76% of the queue returned an unusable value — 67.2% exactly zero, 8.8% no match at all — almost certainly a missing-keyword-match placeholder rather than genuine zero commercial value, since the source paper's own portfolio average for informational content is $0.15, not $0.00. Cost/value reasoning in this study is therefore primarily qualitative.
  • One feature's permutation importance (sessions_organic, value 1.07) was investigated and found to be driven by extreme outliers (its max is 43× its own 99th percentile) rather than a genuinely dominant signal.
  • The decay/refresh timing guidance in the Recommendations below is borrowed from the source research paper's own validated, portfolio-level findings — it was not independently re-derived from this lane's own data, and is disclosed as such.

Required human review, before any action

  • Check spike_day_share above 0.5 against the raw daily rows before trusting the monthly position.
  • Judge whether the low CTR is a fixable title/meta issue or a genuine intent mismatch — this queue cannot tell the two apart.
  • Check the month's data coverage (partial months read less reliably).

Never automate

  • Never auto-publish a title, meta, or content change from this queue.
  • Never use this output for compensation, personnel, or vendor decisions.
  • Never treat a spike_driven_unstable row as confirmed without the raw-data check.

Timing: when to act

The source research paper's own validated lifecycle findings put content's peak performance at 61–90 days and a decay cliff between 271–365 days, with pages refreshed within 30 days of the 365-day mark showing a 3.2× health boost and 57× impression boost in that portfolio. Borrowed, not re-derived here — it argues for reviewing flagged pages promptly rather than leaving them queued, since staleness compounds.

Monitoring — treat this playbook as stale if

  • The position-tier baseline shifts meaningfully month to month.
  • The spike_driven_unstable share grows well beyond 1,167 of 175,304.
  • A repeat forward check shows precision@K drifting back toward the base rate.
  • The client mix changes materially — the tier baseline is computed across all visible clients.
